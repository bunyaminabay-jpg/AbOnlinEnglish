Nominal Clauses — İsim Cümleleri

Nominal (isim) cümleleri, bir cümlede özne, nesne veya tümleç görevini üstlenen yan cümleciklerdir. C1 düzeyi akademik yazımda cümlelere derinlik ve karmaşıklık katar. Türkçe'deki "-dığı, -acağı, -mesi" ekleri gibi işlev görür.

📌 Nominal clause türleri

TürYapıÖrnek
What clauseWhat + S + VWhat concerns me most is the lack of transparency.
That clausethat + S + VIt is clear that action is needed.
The fact thatThe fact that + S + VThe fact that no one was informed is troubling.
Whether clausewhether + S + VThe question is whether we can afford it.
How/Why clausehow/why + S + VHow the data was collected remains unclear.

📌 Özne konumunda nominal clause

What clause — özne
What I find remarkable is her resilience.
What the data shows is deeply worrying.
What we need is a long-term solution.
That / whether — nesne
I believe that the policy is flawed.
She questioned whether the data was reliable.
He admitted that he had been wrong.
💡
TIP — Cleft sentences ile güç
Nominal clause + cleft = C1 seviyesi güç:
Sade: "The timing matters most."
C1: "What matters most is the timing."

Sade: "Lack of funding caused the failure."
C1: "What caused the failure was the lack of funding."

Does Language Shape Thought?

sarı işaretli kelimeler bu ünitenin hedef yapıları ve kalıplarıdır.

What linguists have long debated is whether language shapes thought or merely expresses it — a question far more profound than it might initially appear.

The fact that different languages encode reality in fundamentally different ways gives credence to the hypothesis that language influences cognition. The linguist Benjamin Lee Whorf famously claimed that the Hopi language of the American Southwest has no words or grammatical forms that refer to time as English does — what this implies remains disputed, and later research by Ekkehart Malotki strongly challenged the claim. The Pirahã people of the Amazon speak a language that, according to the controversial research of Daniel Everett, appears to lack recursion entirely — which would mean they cannot embed one thought inside another.

What we take for granted — the assumption that language and thought are entirely separable processes — is precisely what this research calls into question. The extent to which language, culture, and cognition are intertwined remains one of psychology's most genuinely nuanced and unresolved questions.

Some linguists argue the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis is dead, predicated on poor methodology. Others bear out weaker versions of it with careful experiments. Whether colour perception, spatial reasoning, or time orientation can be altered by language structure remains to be established definitively.

It goes without saying that the fact that we are even asking this question reveals something important: what we believe about the relationship between language and mind shapes everything else — our theories of education, consciousness, artificial intelligence, and cross-cultural understanding. What is certain is that the debate is far from over.
