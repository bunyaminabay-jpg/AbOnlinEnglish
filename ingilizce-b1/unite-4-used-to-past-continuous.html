Past Continuous & used to

İki yapı da geçmişle ilgilidir ama farklı amaçlarla kullanılır. Past Continuous geçmişte devam eden bir eylemi, used to ise artık olmayan geçmiş alışkanlıkları anlatır.

📌 Past Continuous — was/were + V-ing

YapıNe zaman?Örnek
was/were + V-ingGeçmişte süren eylemShe was studying when I called.
while + was/were + V-ingİki eş zamanlı eylemWhile I was cooking, he was reading.
when + Simple PastDevam ederken başka şey olduI was walking when it started to rain.

📌 used to — Geçmiş alışkanlıklar

YapıAnlamÖrnek
used to + V1Artık olmayan alışkanlıkI used to walk to school. (artık etmiyorum)
didn't use to + V1Eskiden yapmıyordumShe didn't use to like coffee.
Did ... use to + V1?SoruDid you use to play football?
🔀
DON'T CONFUSE — used to vs would
used to → hem alışkanlık hem durum:
"I used to live in Izmir." (durum — artık orada değilim)
"I used to play tennis." (alışkanlık)

would → sadece geçmiş alışkanlıklar (durum için kullanılmaz!):
✅ "When I was young, I would play football every day."
❌ "I would live in Izmir." → would durum için kullanılmaz!
Past Continuous — hikaye anlatımı
It was raining when she left.
While he was sleeping, the phone rang.
I was making tea when the lights went out.
used to — geçmiş vs şimdi
I used to hate vegetables. (şimdi seviyorum)
She didn't use to wear glasses.
People used to travel by horse. (artık değil)

The Forgetting Curve

sarı işaretli kelimeler bu ünitenin hedef yapıları ve kalıplarıdır.

In 1885, German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus made a disturbing discovery. He was studying his own memory — testing himself with lists of nonsense syllables — when he noticed a worrying pattern.

Without review, he was forgetting more than half of new material within one hour. Within 24 hours, about two-thirds was gone. He called this the "forgetting curve."

Many students today still make the same mistakes Ebbinghaus documented. They used to believe — and many still do — that rereading notes is effective. Research suggests this is not very effective. When students were rereading their notes in studies, they were creating an illusion of knowledge, not genuine learning.

The most effective technique is active recall: closing your book and testing yourself. When psychologists Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke were studying this technique in 2006, they found that students who tested themselves remembered about 56% of a text one week later — compared with 42% for students who simply reread it.

Students used to spend hours highlighting and rereading. Now, the evidence is clear: spaced repetition and retrieval practice — not cramming — are the foundations of lasting memory.

I used to study alone, but now I much prefer studying in groups. When I was younger, I was always forgetting my homework at home. My most effective study technique is the Pomodoro method: 25 minutes of focused work, then a 5-minute break. I discovered it last year when I was struggling with my exams. Before that, I was studying for hours without breaks — it used to be absolutely exhausting. Now I feel much more productive.
