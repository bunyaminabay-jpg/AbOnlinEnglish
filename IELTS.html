Stuck at Band 6?
Here's what Band 7 actually looks like.

Most candidates plateau at 6.0 not from lack of effort, but because Band 7 rewards consistency — using strong vocabulary and grammar throughout, not just in places.

Explore Band 6 → 7
  • Reading: spotting distractors and inference, not just vocabulary matching.
  • Writing: extended, purposeful ideas instead of surface-level coverage of the question.
  • Speaking: sustained fluency and flexible grammar, not occasional complex sentences.
  • Vocabulary: natural collocation — "make a decision," never "do a decision."

The 14-step writing process

Before you write: paragraphs and academic register

Every paragraph you write in Task 1 or Task 2, whatever the topic, is built from the same handful of moving parts. Learn this shape once, alongside the formal register examiners expect it written in, and it pays off across every essay — not just one prompt.

Topic sentenceSupporting idea(s)Example / evidenceConcluding or linking sentence

Formal academic register — do / don't

Do

  • Use hedging language for claims that aren't absolute: "tends to", "is likely to", "arguably", "to some extent".
  • Prefer formal linking devices: "furthermore", "however", "consequently" rather than "also", "but", "so".
  • Write contractions in full: "do not" rather than "don't", "it is" rather than "it's".
  • Choose the more formal synonym where one exists: "obtain" rather than "get", "children" rather than "kids", "investigate" rather than "look into".
  • Use the passive voice where the doer of an action is unimportant or unstated — especially in Task 1 process description.

Don't

  • Don't use contractions ("don't", "can't", "it's") anywhere in either task.
  • Don't lean on informal phrasal verbs where a single-word formal equivalent exists: "find out" → "determine", "look into" → "investigate", "point out" → "indicate".
  • Don't make absolute claims — "always", "never", "everyone", "no one" — without hedging; almost no real-world claim holds true 100% of the time.
  • Don't use informal intensifiers like "really", "a lot of" or "totally" — use "significantly", "considerably" or "a great deal of" instead.
  • Don't address the reader directly ("you can see that…") — keep an impersonal, objective voice throughout.

Task 1 (Academic): the chart-language guide

Task 1 Academic always gives you exactly one visual — a line graph, a bar chart, a pie chart, a table, a process diagram, or a map — and asks you to describe it accurately in your own words. It is a description task, not an argument task, and it is marked on how well you select, organise and language the data, not on how many numbers you manage to mention. Two rules apply to every single chart type below, so they're worth fixing before anything else.

Rule 1 — never include your opinion. Task 1 is purely descriptive; a personal view, a recommendation, or a "this is a worrying trend" aside will not raise your score and can actively hurt Task Achievement, which rewards an accurate, objective report. Rule 2 — overview and key features beat exhaustive detail. The Task Achievement criterion specifically rewards "a clear overview of main trends, differences or stages" and the selection of key features — describing every single data point instead, with no overview, is one of the most common reasons a technically accurate Task 1 answer still scores Band 5–6.
Universal opening lines (all chart types)
  • "The [line graph / bar chart / table / pie chart] illustrates/shows/gives information about [topic], measured in [units], over the period [X–Y]."
  • "According to the [chart type], [one-sentence paraphrase of the single biggest pattern]."
  • "Looking at the [chart type], it can be seen that [one-sentence paraphrase of the single biggest pattern]."

Every Task 1 answer follows the same four-part shape, whichever visual you're given: a paraphrased introduction, an overview naming the one or two most striking overall features, then two body paragraphs working through the supporting detail. Pick a chart type below for its identification cues, its version of that structure, and a full phrase bank organised by function with worked example sentences.

Task 2: the five essay types, heading by heading

Nearly every Task 2 prompt is a variation on one of five underlying essay types, and each type expects a different paragraph structure. Misreading the type is one of the most common — and most avoidable — ways candidates lose Task Response marks: the English can be excellent and still score Band 5–6 if it answers the wrong structural question. Learning to identify the type in the first thirty seconds is worth as much practice time as the writing itself.

If the prompt says……it's asking for
"To what extent do you agree or disagree?" / "Do you agree or disagree?"Opinion
"Discuss both views/these views and give your own opinion."Discussion
"What are the advantages and disadvantages…?" / "…do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?"Advantages/Disadvantages
"What problems does this cause and what solutions can you suggest?"Problem/Solution
Two separate question marks in one prompt (e.g. "Why is this the case? What can be done about it?")Two-Part Question

Cohesion & linking devices, by function

Coherence & Cohesion rewards a natural range of linking devices used accurately — not the frequent repetition of a single favourite. Reusing "however" or "moreover" four or five times in one essay reads as mechanical and is one of the most common band-lowering habits at Band 5–6; keep two or three options ready for each function below and rotate between them.

Adding / listing

furthermore, moreover, in addition, what is more, besides this, on top of that

Contrasting

however, on the other hand, nevertheless, nonetheless, whereas, while, in contrast, despite this

Cause & effect

as a result, consequently, this leads to, due to, as a consequence, this in turn means that

Sequencing

firstly / secondly / finally, subsequently, following this, at this point, prior to this

Exemplifying

for example, for instance, such as, a case in point is, this is illustrated by, namely

Concluding

in conclusion, to sum up, overall, all things considered, taking everything into account

Vary, don't repeat: choose a different linker from the same group each time the function repeats in your essay, and keep the grammar that follows each one in mind — "however" starts a new sentence, "despite this" is followed by a noun phrase or -ing clause, and "whereas" joins two clauses in a single sentence.

Band 5 → Band 8, same prompt

"Some people think children should begin learning a foreign language in primary school, while others believe it is better to wait. Discuss both views and give your own opinion."
