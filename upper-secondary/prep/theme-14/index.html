In Theme 14, you will explore Life in the World and Culture: the capitals of countries, famous landmarks around the globe, and the cultural differences you can find from one city to another. You will learn to report what people said with reported speech, talk about the past with the Past Perfect, imagine what could/should/might have happened with past modals, and talk about future plans with be going to.

Past Perfect: telling a travel story in order

1 · Notice

  • We were surprised because we hadn't visited a capital city before.
  • Had you seen the famous landmark before your trip? — No, I hadn't.
  • When we arrived at the museum, it had just closed.

Which action happened first, and which happened second? How do we form the Past Perfect?

C Your turn

Write three sentences about a trip (real or imaginary): one thing that had already happened before you arrived somewhere, one you hadn't done before, and one question with Had you...?

By the time I arrived, …I hadn't …Had you …?
Rule

We use the Past Perfect (had + V3) for an action that happened before another action in the past — it makes clear which past event came first. Negative: hadn't + V3. Question: Had + subject + V3?

TRPast Perfect (had + V3), geçmişte başka bir geçmiş olaydan önce olan bir eylemi anlatmak için kullanılır: iki geçmiş olay arasında hangisinin önce olduğunu netleştirir. Olumsuzda hadn't + V3, soruda Had + özne + V3 kullanılır.

When?

We use it when telling a travel story and we need to show which of two past events happened first — for example, what had already happened before we arrived.

TRBir seyahat hikâyesini anlatırken, iki geçmiş olayı sırasıyla belirtmek istediğimizde kullanırız: örneğin, biz varmadan önce ne olmuştu.

3 · Form

Positive

  • By the time we arrived, the tour had already started.
  • She had visited three capitals before she turned twenty.

Negative

  • We hadn't seen the landmark before that day.
  • He hadn't tried the local food before his trip.

Question

  • Had you seen the Eiffel Tower before?
  • Had they booked the hotel before they landed?

Short answers

  • Yes, I had. / No, I hadn't.
  • Yes, they had. / No, they hadn't.
Mistakes
  • By the time we arrived, the guide has already started.By the time we arrived, the guide had already started.

    Use had, not has, when sequencing two past events.

    TRİki geçmiş olayı sıralarken has değil had kullanılır.

  • I hadn't visit the capital before.I hadn't visited the capital before.

    Use the past participle (V3) after hadn't, not the base verb.

    TRhadn't'tan sonra V3 (past participle) gelir, yalın fiil değil.

Reported speech (mixed tenses): reporting what people said about their travels

1 · Notice
  • Deniz said, "I visited three capitals last year." → Deniz said (that) she had visited three capitals the year before.
  • Elif said, "I'm going to the pyramids next month." → Elif said (that) she was going to the pyramids the following month.
  • Mert said, "The landmark is amazing." → Mert said (that) the landmark was amazing.
  • The guide said, "I have worked here for ten years." → The guide said (that) he had worked there for ten years.

How does the verb tense change when we report what someone said? What happens to words like 'next month' or 'here'?

C Your turn

Think of something a friend or family member once said about a trip or a place. Write three reported-speech sentences about it, changing the tense correctly.

… said (that) …… told me (that) …
Rule

In reported speech, the verb usually moves one tense back: Present Simple → Past Simple, Past Simple → Past Perfect, be going to → was/were going to, Present Perfect → Past Perfect. Time and place words also change: now → then, here → there, tomorrow → the next day.

TRReported speech'te (dolaylı anlatım), söylenen cümleyi kendi cümlemizmiş gibi aktarırken fiil genellikle bir zaman geriye kayar: Present Simple → Past Simple, Past Simple → Past Perfect, be going to → was/were going to, Present Perfect → Past Perfect. Zaman/yer zarfları da değişir: now→then, here→there, tomorrow→the next day.

When?

We use it when telling someone else what a person said about their travels, cultures or places, and it requires mixing different past tenses correctly.

TRBirinin seyahatler, kültürler veya yerler hakkında söylediklerini başka birine aktarırken kullanılır; farklı geçmiş zamanların doğru sırayla kullanılmasını gerektirir.

3 · Form

Positive

  • She said (that) she had visited the capital.
  • He said (that) he was going to travel next year.

Negative

  • They said (that) they hadn't tried the local food.
  • She said (that) she didn't like the crowded landmark.

Question

  • I asked her if/whether she had enjoyed the trip.

Short answers

  • She said she had. / He said he hadn't.
Mistakes
  • She said that she visits the capital every year.She said that she visited the capital every year.

    Present Simple usually shifts back to Past Simple.

    TRPresent Simple genellikle Past Simple'a kayar.

  • He said he is going to travel next year.He said he was going to travel the following year.

    be going to shifts back to was/were going to; next year becomes the following year.

    TRbe going to → was/were going to şeklinde kayar; next year → the following year olur.

Compare

Reported speech'te zaman genellikle bir adım geriye kayar, ama söylenen şey hâlâ doğruysa (örneğin genel bir gerçek) bazen zaman değişmeyebilir: 'The Earth is round' → She said the Earth is/was round.

C Your turn

Think of a travel mistake (real or imaginary). Write three sentences: one with should have (what you should have done), one with could have (another option), and one with might have (a guess about what happened).

I should have …I could have …Maybe I might have …
Rule

Modal + have + V3 is used for things in the past that didn't happen, or that we are not sure about. Should have: something that didn't happen but should have (regret/criticism). Could have: a past possibility that didn't happen. Might/may have: an unsure guess about the past. Would have: imagining what would have happened in a different past situation.

TRModal + have + V3 yapısı, geçmişte gerçekleşmemiş ya da gerçekleşip gerçekleşmediğinden emin olmadığımız durumlar için kullanılır. Should have: geçmişte yapılmayan ama yapılması gereken bir şey (pişmanlık/eleştiri). Could have: geçmişte mümkün olan ama olmayan bir seçenek. Might/may have: geçmiş hakkında emin olmayan bir tahmin. Would have: farklı bir geçmiş durumda ne olacağını hayal etme.

When?

We use these to criticise a travel mistake, talk about a missed opportunity, or guess about something that might have happened in the past.

TRBir seyahat hatasını eleştirirken, kaçırılan bir fırsatı anlatırken ya da geçmişte olabilecek bir şeyi tahmin ederken kullanırız.

Capitals That Might Surprise You

When people think of a capital city, they often imagine the biggest, busiest city in the country. But that isn't always true. In some countries, the capital is a smaller, quieter administrative city, built specially to be the seat of government.

Brazil is a good example. Rio de Janeiro used to be the capital, but in 1960 the government had already decided to relocate the capital to a new city called Brasília. City planners had designed Brasília from nothing, in the shape of an aeroplane, in the middle of the country. Today, government buildings, embassies and diplomats fill the city, while Rio remains the more famous tourist destination.

Another surprising example is Switzerland. Many people guess that Zürich, the biggest and richest city, is the capital. In fact, Bern, a much smaller city with a lower population, holds that role. Bern's old town, with its narrow streets and medieval towers, is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Turkey has a similar story. Istanbul is the largest city and the main cultural and economic centre, but Ankara has been the official capital since 1923, when the young republic had chosen it as a more central administrative city.

So, before you travel, it's worth checking: is the capital the city everyone talks about, or is it a quieter place with a very different kind of importance?

The Landmark and the Story Behind It

Millions of tourists visit the Eiffel Tower every year, but most don't know it was almost destroyed. In 1889, the tower had been built as a temporary entrance for the World's Fair in Paris. Many people at the time thought it was ugly, and the plan was to take it down after twenty years.

By the time the twenty years had passed, engineers had already found a new use for the tower: radio transmission. Because it was so tall, it was perfect for sending radio signals across the city. This breathtaking piece of engineering had earned itself a permanent place in Paris.

Today, the tower attracts around seven million visitors a year, making it one of the most-visited paid landmarks in the world. Tourists climb it, take photographs from below, and watch it sparkle with lights every evening.

This story shows something interesting about landmarks: sometimes they aren't built to become symbols. Their history, use, and the culture around them can change how people see a place over time.

So next time you take a photo next to a famous monument, it might be worth asking: what was this place originally built for, and how did it become so important?

Planning Our Ideal World Tour

1 Before listening

  • You will hear two friends planning a world tour for a school project.
  • Predict: which two or three capital cities do you think they might choose?
0:00

2 While listening

Transkripti göster

AdaSo for our project, we're going to design our ideal world tour. Where should we start?

KeremI've always wanted to visit Tokyo. I read that it had already become one of the biggest cities in the world by the 1960s.

AdaGreat choice. After Tokyo, we could visit Cairo — I'd love to see the pyramids. They're ancient, and the construction is amazing for that time.

KeremGood idea. And we should have added Rome from the start — I can't believe we almost forgot it!

AdaYou're right, we should have. Let's add it as our third city. Rome has so many landmarks — the Colosseum, for example.

KeremPerfect. And for the cultural differences part, we could compare greetings — my cousin told me that in Japan people bow instead of shaking hands.

AdaThat's a really good example. We're going to write about three capitals, their landmarks, and their customs. This is going to be a fantastic project!

KeremDefinitely. Let's start writing the script for our characters tonight.

3 After listening

  • If you designed your own ideal world tour, which three cities would you choose? Why?
  • Write one sentence with 'should have' about a plan you almost forgot.

A Cultural Misunderstanding

1 Before listening

  • You will hear a conversation about a mix-up caused by cultural differences.
  • Predict: what kind of misunderstanding might happen when someone visits a new country?
0:00

2 While listening

Transkripti göster

ZeynepYou'll never guess what happened when I visited my friend's family in South Korea!

BurakWhat happened?

ZeynepWell, I hadn't researched the etiquette before I went, so I poured my own drink at dinner. My friend told me later that in Korea, you usually let someone else pour your drink for you.

BurakOh no! You should have asked her about the customs first.

ZeynepI know, I should have! I might have offended her grandmother, but luckily she just laughed and explained the tradition.

BurakThat's a relief. Did anything else surprise you?

ZeynepYes — I found out that people often remove their shoes before entering a house there, which is different from here.

BurakInteresting. I think our project should have a section just about these small cultural differences — they're fascinating.

ZeynepAgreed. I'm going to write about this experience for the writing task.

3 After listening

  • Have you ever experienced or heard about a cultural misunderstanding? Describe it in two sentences.
  • Write one sentence with 'should have' about researching customs before a trip.

SpeakingWhole class, mingling10-12 dk

Find Someone Who… Has Travel Dreams

Walk around the class and ask your classmates questions with Are you going to…?, Have you ever…? and Would you like to…? to find someone who matches each sentence about world travel. Write their name next to the sentence.

Useful language

Are you going to visit a capital city this year?Have you ever seen a famous landmark in real life?Would you like to try food from a different culture?Yes, I am. / No, I'm not. / Yes, I have. / No, I haven't.

Adımlar

  1. Look at your worksheet: 6 sentences about world travel dreams (e.g. '…wants to visit three capitals one day').
  2. Stand up and ask a classmate a question to check if the sentence is true for them.
  3. If it's true, write their name. If not, ask another classmate.
  4. Try to find a different name for each sentence.
  5. Sit down and share two interesting answers with the class.
DestekGive students the question forms in writing before they start.
Hedef6 questions, mingling for about 10 minutes.
İleriFast finishers add one more true sentence about their own travel dreams and ask it too.
SpeakingGroups of 3-412-15 dk

Pitch Your Ideal World Tour

In your group, pitch your ideal world tour project idea to another group: which capitals you are going to include, one landmark for each, and one cultural difference you found interesting. Use reported speech to share something a classmate told you during research.

Useful language

We are going to visit…One landmark we chose is…A classmate told me that…We should have researched… before we decided.

Adımlar

  1. In your group, choose three capital cities for your ideal tour.
  2. Decide on one famous landmark to include for each city.
  3. Agree on one cultural difference between the cities that you find interesting.
  4. Practise your pitch, using at least two grammar points from this theme.
  5. Present your pitch to another group and answer their questions.
DestekProvide a simple pitch template with sentence starters for lower-level students.
Hedef3 capitals, 3 landmarks, 1 cultural difference, full group pitch.
İleriFast groups add a fourth city and explain why they chose it.
Writing80-100 words

Two Capitals, Two Stories

Write a short guide comparing two capital cities you find interesting. Describe one landmark in each city and one cultural difference between them. Use at least two grammar points from this theme (Past Perfect, reported speech, past modals, be going to).

Plan

  1. Choose two capital cities to compare.
  2. Note one famous landmark for each city.
  3. Note one cultural difference between the two places.
  4. Check your grammar and punctuation.

Writing100-120 words

My Ideal World Tour Project Plan

Write a first draft plan for the performance task: your ideal world tour project. Describe which capitals, landmarks and cultural differences you are going to focus on, and briefly introduce one character who represents a country in your project.

Plan

  1. List the capitals and landmarks you are going to include.
  2. Note one cultural difference you want to show.
  3. Introduce one character (name, country, one thing they say or do).
  4. Read your plan aloud and check it makes sense as a starting draft.
