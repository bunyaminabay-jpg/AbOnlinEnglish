1 · Notice

  • The flood that destroyed the village changed the way the government plans for disasters.
  • This is the kind of technology which helps doctors diagnose diseases faster.
  • People who live near the coast are usually the most vulnerable to storms.

C Your turn

Write four sentences about natural disasters or technology, each with a defining relative clause (who/which/that).

Scientists who...The disaster that...A system which...People who...
Rule

A defining relative clause gives essential information about the noun in the main clause; without it, the sentence's meaning would be incomplete or unclear. Use who/that for people and which/that for things. No commas are used with defining relative clauses.

TRDefining (tanımlayıcı) relative clause, ana cümledeki isim hakkında zorunlu/temel bilgi verir; bu bilgi olmadan cümlenin anlamı eksik veya belirsiz kalır. İnsanlar için who/that, nesneler için which/that kullanılır. Bu tür cümlelerde virgül KULLANILMAZ.

When?

We use defining relative clauses when we need to make clear exactly which person or thing we are talking about — for example, identifying one specific disaster among many.

TRHangi kişi ya da nesneden bahsettiğimizi netleştirmek istediğimizde defining relative clause kullanırız — örneğin birçok deprem arasından belirli birini tanımlarken.

C Your turn

Write three sentences about people or places you know, adding extra (non-defining) information with who/which and commas.

My..., who..., ......, which..., is/was...
Rule

A non-defining relative clause adds extra information about the noun; the sentence still makes complete sense without it. Commas are used before and after the clause, and 'that' is NOT used in non-defining clauses.

TRNon-defining (tanımlayıcı olmayan) relative clause, ana cümledeki isim hakkında EK/fazladan bilgi verir; bu bilgi çıkarılsa bile cümle hâlâ anlamlıdır. Cümlenin başında ve sonunda VİRGÜL kullanılır ve bu tür cümlelerde 'that' KULLANILMAZ.

When?

We use non-defining relative clauses to add an interesting or extra detail about a noun whose identity is already clear (proper names, unique people or places).

TRZaten kim/ne olduğu belli olan bir isim (özel isimler, tek olan yer/kişiler) hakkında ilginç ya da ek bir detay eklemek istediğimizde non-defining relative clause kullanırız.

C Your turn

Write two sentences with an object relative clause where you can omit the pronoun, and one sentence with a subject relative clause where you cannot.

The ... I ...The ... that/which ... (subject) ...
Rule

The relative pronoun (that/which/who) can usually be omitted when it is the OBJECT of the relative clause (the clause has its own subject). It cannot be omitted when it is the SUBJECT of the clause. This rule only applies to defining relative clauses.

TRRelative pronoun (that/which/who), relative clause içinde NESNE görevindeyse (clause'un kendi öznesi varsa) genellikle atlanabilir. Ancak relative pronoun clause'un ÖZNESİ ise atlanamaz. Bu kural sadece defining relative clause'lar için geçerlidir.

When?

We often omit the object relative pronoun in speech and everyday writing to make sentences sound more natural and less formal.

TRKonuşma dilinde ve günlük yazıda cümleyi daha akıcı hale getirmek için nesne konumundaki relative pronoun'u atlarız.

C Your turn

Write three sentences with 'whose', describing people, places or organisations connected to natural disasters or technology.

The scientist whose...A family whose...The city whose...
Rule

'Whose' is a possessive relative pronoun used to show that something belongs to a person or thing already mentioned. It can be used for people AND things/places, and it is always followed by a noun (whose + noun).

TR'Whose', bir kişi ya da şeye ait olan bir başka isim hakkında bilgi vermek için kullanılan iyelik bildiren relative pronoun'dur. Hem insanlar hem de nesneler/yerler için kullanılabilir ve kendisinden sonra mutlaka bir isim gelir (whose + isim).

When?

We use 'whose' to introduce a feature that belongs to a person or place — their house, family, system, and so on.

TRBir kişinin ya da yerin sahip olduğu bir özelliği (evi, ailesi, sistemi vb.) tanıtırken 'whose' kullanırız.

When the Ground Shakes: The Real Cost of a Natural Disaster

At 4:17 in the morning, the small town of Elmalı, which had never experienced a serious earthquake before, began to shake violently for almost forty seconds. Buildings that had stood for decades collapsed within minutes, and the streets, which were usually silent at that hour, filled with dust, noise and frightened residents searching for their families. By sunrise, rescue teams whose job it was to search the rubble had already pulled dozens of survivors from collapsed buildings, but for many families the search continued for days.

The disaster's impact on the surrounding countryside was just as severe as its impact on the town itself. The earthquake triggered several landslides that blocked mountain roads and changed the course of a small river, flooding farmland that had fed the community for generations. Forests on the hillsides, whose roots normally hold the soil in place, were badly damaged, which made the land even more vulnerable to future landslides. Local wildlife, including birds and small mammals that depended on those forests, lost much of their natural habitat almost overnight.

In the weeks that followed, thousands of people who had lost their homes were moved to temporary shelters, and this displacement created its own challenges: overcrowding, limited clean water, and the stress of an uncertain future. Yet the relief effort that followed also showed remarkable resilience. Volunteers whose only connection to Elmalı was a desire to help arrived from across the country, and within a month a temporary school, which the community built almost entirely by hand, was already open for local children.

Experts who study natural disasters point out that Elmalı's experience is far from unique. Every year, similar events devastate communities that are simply unprepared for what nature can do — a reminder that understanding both the human and the environmental cost of a disaster is the first step towards being ready for the next one.

Can Technology Predict the Next Disaster?

For most of human history, natural disasters arrived almost without warning. Today, however, artificial intelligence and data analysis are changing that completely. Scientists who study climate patterns now use algorithms that can process huge amounts of satellite data, weather records and historical information — far more than any human team could analyse alone — to identify the early signs of an earthquake, flood or wildfire before it happens.

One clear example is the early warning system that many coastal cities have started to install. Sensors, which are placed along riverbanks and coastlines, constantly send data to a central system whose algorithm compares current conditions with thousands of past events. When the pattern matches a known danger, the system can send an alert to residents' phones minutes — or sometimes hours — before a flood or a tsunami arrives. In regions prone to wildfires, similar technology uses satellite images to detect the first signs of smoke, often long before anyone on the ground notices anything unusual.

The benefits of this technology are not limited to prediction. After a disaster strikes, hospitals whose emergency departments would otherwise be overwhelmed can use data models to estimate how many patients they are likely to receive and what kind of injuries to expect, allowing them to prepare staff and supplies more effectively. Humanitarian organisations, which used to rely mainly on phone calls and paper reports, now use real-time data to decide where relief teams are needed most urgently.

Of course, technology is not a perfect solution. Systems that depend on expensive sensors and reliable internet connections are often unavailable in the poorest and most vulnerable communities — the very places that frequently need them most. Experts agree that AI and data can dramatically reduce the damage a disaster causes, but only if the people who design these systems remember to make them available to everyone, not just to wealthy cities that can already afford them.
