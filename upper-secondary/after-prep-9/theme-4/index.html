Present Perfect vs Present Perfect Progressive

1 · Notice
  • I have lived in this flat since 2019.
  • My grandmother has been living with us for six months now, and she loves it.
  • We have already tidied the living room, so we can relax now.
  • My brother has been cleaning his room all morning, but it still looks messy.

Which sentences focus on the result or completion of an action, and which focus on the duration of an ongoing activity? Which time expressions (since, for, already, all morning) go with each form?

C Your turn

Write four sentences about your own family or home: one thing you have already done today, one thing you have never done, and one activity you have been doing for a while (use 'for' or 'since').

I have already…I have never…I have been … for/since…My family has lived here for/since…
Rule

The Present Perfect (have/has + past participle) describes a completed action or its result, connected to now (used with just, already, yet, ever, never, recently). The Present Perfect Progressive (have/has been + -ing) emphasises the DURATION of an action that started in the past and continues now, or continued until recently (often with for/since).

TRPresent Perfect (have/has + fiilin 3. hali), geçmişte olmuş ve şu anla bağlantısı olan tamamlanmış bir eylemi ya da onun sonucunu anlatır (just, already, yet, ever, never, recently ile kullanılır). Present Perfect Progressive (have/has been + -ing) ise geçmişte başlayıp devam eden ya da yakın zamana kadar süren bir eylemin SÜRESİNE vurgu yapar (genellikle for/since ile).

When?

Use the Present Perfect for a completed action or an experience (e.g. household chores); use the Present Perfect Progressive to emphasise how long a family has lived somewhere or how long an activity has continued.

TREv işleri gibi tamamlanmış bir eylemden veya deneyimden bahsederken Present Perfect; bir ailenin ne kadar süredir bir yerde yaşadığını ya da bir eylemin ne kadar sürdüğünü vurgularken Present Perfect Progressive kullanırız.

    TRSüregelen bir eylemin süresini vurgularken Present Perfect Progressive kullanılır.

    TR'share' burada edilgen değil etken kullanılır; sadece 3. hali gelir.

Comparative and Superlative of Irregular Adjectives

1 · Notice
  • Life in a shared flat can be worse than life alone when you have no privacy.

Rule

Some adjectives have irregular comparative and superlative forms: good-better-best, bad-worse-worst, far-farther/further-farthest/furthest, little-less-least, many/much-more-most. These do not take -er/-est or more/most — the whole word changes.

TRBazı sıfatların karşılaştırma ve üstünlük halleri düzensizdir: good-better-best, bad-worse-worst, far-farther/further-farthest/furthest, little-less-least, many/much-more-most. Bu sıfatlara -er/-est eklenmez ya da more/most getirilmez; formları tamamen değişir.

When?

We use these irregular forms when comparing homes, family situations and relationships — for example, which house is better, or who has less patience.

TREv, aile ve komşuluk ilişkilerini karşılaştırırken (örneğin hangi ev daha iyi, kimin sabrı daha az) bu düzensiz biçimleri kullanırız.

C Your turn

Write three true sentences about you or your family using reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself, himself/herself, ourselves, themselves).

I sometimes … myself.My … taught … himself/herself …We enjoyed ourselves when …
Rule

Reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves) are used when the subject and the object of a sentence are the same person or thing. 'By + reflexive pronoun' means 'alone, without help'.

TRDönüşlü zamirler (myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves), cümlenin öznesi ile nesnesi aynı kişi/şey olduğunda kullanılır. 'By + dönüşlü zamir' kalıbı 'yardımsız, tek başına' anlamına gelir.

When?

We use reflexive pronouns to describe an action someone does to themselves, or to say that someone does something alone/without help.

TRBir kişinin kendine yaptığı bir eylemi, ya da bir şeyi tek başına/yardımsız yaptığını anlatırken dönüşlü zamir kullanırız.

  • Yes, they can. / No, not yet.
Mistakes
  • My grandmother lives by her.My grandmother lives by herself.

    Use 'by + reflexive pronoun', not 'by + object pronoun'.

    TR'by' ile kişi zamiri değil, dönüşlü zamir kullanılır.

  • They enjoyed theirselves at the party.They enjoyed themselves at the party.

    The correct plural reflexive pronoun is 'themselves' — 'theirselves' is not a word.

    TRDoğru çoğul dönüşlü zamir 'themselves'dir, 'theirselves' diye bir kelime yoktur.

  • We each other help with the chores.We help each other with the chores.

    'each other' expresses a reciprocal action (not reflexive) and comes after the verb.

    TR'each other' karşılıklı eylem için kullanılır (dönüşlü zamir değildir) ve fiilden sonra gelir.

Rule

'How long' asks about duration. It is followed by the Present Perfect (Simple/Progressive) for something continuing up to now, the Past Simple for a completed period in the past, and will/going to for a future duration.

TR'How long' süre sormak için kullanılır. Şu ana kadar devam eden bir durum için Present Perfect (Simple/Progressive), tamamlanmış bir geçmiş süre için Past Simple, gelecekteki bir süre için will/going to ile kullanılır.

When?

We use 'How long' to ask how long a family has lived somewhere, how long an argument lasted, or how long a visit will continue.

TRAilenin bir evde ne kadar süredir yaşadığını, bir tartışmanın ne kadar sürdüğünü ya da bir ziyaretin ne kadar süreceğini sorarken 'How long' kullanırız.

Homes Around the World

Step outside your own front door and you will find a huge variety of homes across the globe, each shaped by climate, geography and culture. In Mongolia, many nomadic families still live in a ger, a round, portable tent made of felt and wood that can be packed up and moved within a few hours when herders travel to new grazing land. In Hong Kong, by contrast, more than seven million people live in one of the most densely populated cities on Earth, and most families share small, efficient apartments stacked dozens of storeys high above narrow streets.

In parts of Southeast Asia and West Africa, stilt houses raise the living space above rivers, floodwater or simply the damp ground, keeping homes cooler and safer from pests. In the Netherlands, some families have chosen to live on the water itself, in houseboats moored along the canals — a practical answer to a small, flat country with limited land. Meanwhile, in the suburbs of many Western cities, detached houses with private gardens remain the dream for families who value space and privacy above all else.

These differences are rarely accidental. A home's shape, size and materials usually reflect what is available locally, how extended a family typically is, and how much space a culture expects each generation to share. Understanding housing diversity, then, is really a way of understanding how people everywhere adapt to their environment — and to each other.

Three Generations, One Roof

When Zeynep's grandparents moved in with her family two years ago, the house suddenly felt smaller — and louder. Zeynep, her parents, her younger brother, and now her grandparents all share one kitchen, one living room, and, most of the time, one opinion about how loudly the television should be played. 'At first, it was strange,' Zeynep admits. 'My grandmother wanted dinner at six, my parents preferred eight, and nobody could agree on the thermostat.'

Daily conflicts like these are common in multi-generational households, where different generations often have different habits, schedules and expectations about privacy. Zeynep's family found that the biggest arguments weren't really about dinner times or the television — they were about respect and personal space. Her parents felt that they had lost some control over their own home; her grandparents felt that their advice, built on decades of experience, was not always welcome.

Slowly, the family developed a few simple house rules: everyone gets one hour of quiet time in the evening, chores are divided fairly between the generations, and big decisions — from meals to holidays — are discussed together at a short Sunday meeting. 'It's not perfect,' says Zeynep, 'but we've learned to compromise, and honestly, having my grandparents here has been the best thing that's happened to our family. My brother and I have learned so much from them, and they don't feel lonely anymore.'

Speaking

Comparing Homes Around the World

In pairs, compare your own home with one of the housing types from Reading 1 (a ger, an apartment block, a stilt house or a houseboat). Talk about size, location, and what you like or dislike about each. Use at least one irregular comparative/superlative and one 'How long' question.

Speaking

A Day in My Multi-Generational Family

Prepare a two-minute talk describing a typical day in your own household (or an imaginary multi-generational household). Mention at least one small daily conflict and how the family solves it, and describe how your family stays connected to your neighbourhood or community.

Writing

Housing Around the World: A Comparison

Write a short report (150–180 words) comparing two different types of housing from around the world (for example, from Reading 1, or your own research). Describe size, materials, location, and typical family life in each, and give your own opinion about which you would prefer.
