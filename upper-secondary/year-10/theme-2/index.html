This theme looks at everyday classroom life and learning: what students do in lessons, how they prefer to learn, and how technology helps them study, while building the grammar needed to talk about future plans, advice, obligation, and possibility.

Rule

Use will for decisions made at the moment of speaking, promises, offers, and predictions without evidence. Use be going to for plans already decided before speaking and predictions based on present evidence.

TRKonuşma anında verilen kararlar, sözler, teklifler ve kanıta dayanmayan tahminler için 'will' kullanılır. Konuşmadan önce zaten karar verilmiş planlar ve mevcut kanıta dayanan tahminler için 'be going to' kullanılır.

When?

We use will for spontaneous decisions ('I'll answer that') and be going to for plans ('I'm going to study tonight').

TRAni kararlar için 'will' ('Ben cevaplarım'), planlar için 'be going to' ('Bu akşam ders çalışacağım') kullanılır.

Rule

Use should/ought to for advice and recommendations. Use must for strong personal obligation or rules from the speaker, and have to for obligation coming from outside (a rule, a teacher, a schedule). Add a question tag to a statement to check information: positive statement + negative tag, negative statement + positive tag.

TRTavsiye ve öneri için 'should/ought to' kullanılır. Konuşanın kendi koyduğu güçlü bir zorunluluk için 'must', dışarıdan gelen bir zorunluluk (kural, öğretmen, program) için 'have to' kullanılır. Bilgiyi teyit etmek için cümlenin sonuna bir soru eki eklenir: olumlu cümle + olumsuz soru eki, olumsuz cümle + olumlu soru eki.

When?

We use should/ought to when giving advice, must for strong rules or feelings, have to for outside obligation, and question tags when checking or confirming information in conversation.

TRTavsiye verirken 'should/ought to', güçlü kurallar veya kişisel görüşler için 'must', dışarıdan gelen zorunluluk için 'have to', konuşmada bilgi teyit ederken soru ekleri kullanılır.

Rule

Reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves) show that the subject and object of an action are the same. Possessive pronouns (mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs) and 's show ownership. Can is used for ability, permission, requests, or possibility. Might is used for a weaker, less certain possibility than may or can.

TRDönüşlü zamirler (myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves) eylemin öznesi ve nesnesinin aynı kişi olduğunu gösterir. İyelik zamirleri (mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs) ve 's eki sahiplik bildirir. 'Can' yetenek, izin, rica veya olasılık için kullanılır. 'Might', 'may' veya 'can'e göre daha zayıf, daha az kesin bir olasılık bildirir.

When?

We use reflexive pronouns when the subject does the action to itself, possessives to avoid repeating a noun, can for everyday requests/permission, and might when we are not sure something will happen.

TRÖzne eylemi kendine yaparken dönüşlü zamir, bir ismi tekrar etmemek için iyelik zamiri, günlük rica/izinlerde 'can', bir şeyin olup olmayacağından emin olunmadığında 'might' kullanılır.

A Day in Our Classroom

Every morning, our classroom is full of different learning activities. In the first lesson, we usually work in pairs and check our homework together. Our teacher walks around the room, listens to our conversations, and gives quick feedback when we make a mistake. Some students prefer working alone, but most of us enjoy pair work because we can help each other understand new words.

Later in the day, we often do a short presentation. Each group chooses a topic, prepares a few slides, and speaks in front of the class. At first, many students feel nervous, but after a few presentations, they become more confident. Presentations are also a great way to practise pronunciation and organise ideas clearly.

In the afternoon, we sometimes use a tablet or a computer for an online quiz. This is going to be a normal part of every lesson soon, because our school is going to buy more digital devices next year. Some students are visual learners and remember new grammar better when they see a diagram on the screen, while others are auditory learners and prefer listening to a short recording.

At the end of the day, the teacher gives us a handout with revision notes and says, 'You should read this at home tonight.' Not every student agrees on the best learning style, but everyone must complete the homework by the next lesson. For most of us, a mix of pair work, presentations, and technology makes learning more interesting than just reading from a book.

Finding Your Own Learning Style

Not every student learns in the same way, and understanding your own learning style can make studying easier. Some people are visual learners: they remember information better when they see it as a picture, colour, or diagram. Other people are auditory learners, who understand a new grammar point more easily after listening to an explanation or a recording rather than reading it silently.

There is also a third common style, sometimes called hands-on or kinaesthetic learning. Students with this style often say that they learn best by doing something practical, such as acting out a dialogue, building a model, or moving around the classroom during an activity. If you often find yourself doodling in the margin of your notebook, you might actually be a hands-on learner who needs movement to concentrate.

Of course, most students are not only one type. You might be mainly a visual learner but still enjoy listening to a short podcast, and you should try different methods before deciding what works best for you. A simple way to discover your style is to keep a short learning diary for two weeks and write down which activities helped you remember new vocabulary or grammar most easily.

Teachers can support different learning styles by mixing activities: showing a diagram for visual learners, playing a short recording for auditory learners, and adding a hands-on task like a role-play for kinaesthetic learners. As a student, you don't have to wait for the teacher to do this — you can also adapt your own revision at home, for example by turning your notes into a mind map, a short recording, or flashcards you can hold and sort.

Classroom Rules Role-Play

In groups, agree on five classroom rules for using technology, using must/mustn't and have to, then present your rules to the class.

Study Tips for a New Classmate

Write a short set of study tips for a new classmate joining your English class, using should/ought to and must/have to appropriately.

Plan

  1. Think of 3-4 useful study habits.
  2. Decide which are advice (should) and which are rules (must/have to).
  3. Order your tips logically.
