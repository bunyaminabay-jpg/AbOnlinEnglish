C Your turn

Write 2-3 original sentences about your family's past using was/were or there was/were.

When I was younger, ...There was/were ... in my grandparents' house.
Rule

We use was/were as the past form of the verb 'be' to describe states, people or things in the past. There was/there were describes something that existed in the past.

TR'Be' fiilinin geçmiş hali olan was/were, geçmişteki durumları, kişileri veya nesneleri anlatmak için kullanılır. There was/there were ise geçmişte var olan bir şeyi anlatmak için kullanılır.

When?

Use was with I/he/she/it and were with you/we/they. Use there was/were to say something existed or happened.

TRI/he/she/it ile was, you/we/they ile were kullanılır. There was/were bir şeyin var olduğunu veya gerçekleştiğini söylemek için kullanılır.

Rule

A question tag is a short question added to the end of a statement to check information or ask for agreement. A positive statement takes a negative tag, and a negative statement takes a positive tag.

TRQuestion tag, bir bilgiyi doğrulamak veya onay istemek için cümle sonuna eklenen kısa bir sorudur. Olumlu bir cümle olumsuz bir tag alır, olumsuz bir cümle ise olumlu bir tag alır.

When?

We use question tags in spoken English to confirm information we think is true or to invite the listener to agree.

TRQuestion tag'ler, doğru olduğunu düşündüğümüz bir bilgiyi teyit etmek veya karşımızdaki kişinin onayını almak için konuşma dilinde kullanılır.

TRCould/couldn't, geçmişte birinin sahip olduğu veya olmadığı genel yetenekleri anlatmak için kullanılır.

When?

Use could for a general past ability, not for one specific successful action (for that we use managed to / was able to).

TRCould, geçmişteki genel bir yeteneği anlatmak için kullanılır; tek seferlik başarılı bir eylem için managed to / was able to kullanılır.

The Yılmaz Family: Then and Now

The Yılmaz family lives in a busy apartment in Izmir, but their daily routine looks very different from their grandparents' routine fifty years ago.

Today, Mrs Yılmaz wakes up at six thirty, checks her phone, and drives her children to school before going to her office. Her husband works from home three days a week and cooks dinner using recipes he finds online. Their two children, Deniz and Elif, have busy afternoons: Deniz plays football twice a week, and Elif takes a coding class on Saturdays.

Fifty years ago, Mrs Yılmaz's grandparents used to live in a small village outside the city. There was no electricity in the house until the 1970s, and there wasn't a television for many years. Her grandfather used to get up before sunrise to work on the family's small farm, and her grandmother used to bake bread every morning. The children walked to school, and in the evening the whole extended family sat together and told stories, because there wasn't much else to do for entertainment.

One thing that hasn't changed, though, is Sunday lunch. Just like fifty years ago, the Yılmaz family still gathers every Sunday, and relatives from nearby towns often join them. "My grandmother could cook the most delicious dishes with very simple ingredients," says Mrs Yılmaz. "I try to keep some of her recipes alive, even though our lives are completely different now."

Deniz says he sometimes finds it hard to imagine life without the internet, but he loves listening to his great-grandmother's stories about the old village house and its enormous garden.

Homes and Families Around the World

Families and homes look very different from country to country, and each type of home tells us something about the people who live there.

In Japan, many urban families live in small, well-organised apartments, often just outside big cities like Tokyo. Homes are usually compact, so families use clever storage and furniture that can be folded away, such as low tables and futon beds. Traditionally, several generations used to live together, but today many young couples prefer their own smaller home close to their parents.

In Morocco, many families live in a riad, a traditional house built around a beautiful inner courtyard. In the past, riads were built for extended families, with grandparents, parents, aunts, uncles and cousins all living together under one roof. There wasn't a strict separation between generations; everyone shared meals and celebrations in the central courtyard.

In Norway, detached wooden houses, called "hytte" for holiday homes, are extremely common, and many nuclear families own a small cabin in the mountains or by the sea. Norwegian families often spend weekends there, hiking, skiing or simply relaxing away from the city.

In Nigeria, extended family networks are very important, and it is common for grandparents, uncles, aunts and cousins to live in the same compound, a group of houses built close together around a shared yard. Children often grow up surrounded by many relatives, and neighbours are treated almost like family members.

These examples show that although family structures and homes are different around the world, the value families place on being together often stays the same.
