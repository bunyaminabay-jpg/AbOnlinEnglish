Expressing Free Choice: Whoever, Whatever, Whichever, No Matter...

1 · Notice
  • Whoever created that phishing email clearly wanted to trick people into giving away their passwords.
  • You can choose whichever password manager you trust most — the important thing is that you use one.
  • No matter what a scammer promises you online, never share your bank details by email.
  • Whatever method hackers use, a strong firewall and common sense can stop most attacks.

Which words let you talk about any person, thing or choice at all, without limiting it to one specific one?

2 · Discover

A Complete the rule

Look at the Notice sentences again. Complete the rule with the words in the box.

any personany thingany one you chooseit makes no difference

Exactly — you've worked out how these free-choice expressions work!

B Build sentences

Use the cues to build true or logical sentences using a free-choice expression.

  1. you can install / whichever antivirus program you prefer

    ✓ You can install whichever antivirus program you prefer.

  2. the police will investigate / whoever is behind the data breach
    Model answer

    The police will investigate whoever is behind the data breach.

  3. no matter what / a stranger online / promise you never / send money
    Model answer

    No matter what a stranger online promises you, never send money.

  4. you can report the scam / to whichever authority handles cybercrime in your country
    Model answer

    You can report the scam to whichever authority handles cybercrime in your country.

  5. whatever / password / you choose make it strong and unique
    Model answer

    Whatever password you choose, make it strong and unique.

  6. ? / whoever / send / this phishing email know your email address
    Model answer

    Does whoever sent this phishing email know your email address?

C Your turn

Write four sentences about online safety using whoever, whatever, whichever and no matter what/who.

Whoever…Whatever…You can choose whichever…No matter what…
Rule

Whoever, whatever and whichever mean 'any person/thing/one you choose', without limiting it to one specific option. No matter who/what/which have the same meaning: 'it makes no difference which one'.

TRWhoever (kim olursa olsun), whatever (ne olursa olsun), whichever (hangisini seçerseniz seçin) ve no matter who/what/which (kim/ne/hangisi olursa olsun) ifadeleri, belirli bir kişi, şey ya da seçenekle sınırlı kalmadan 'her kim/her ne/hangisi olursa olsun' anlamı vermek için kullanılır.

When?

We use these expressions when a rule or warning applies to everyone/everything, or when there is free choice between several options — especially in online-safety advice.

TRBir kuralın ya da uyarının herkes/her şey için geçerli olduğunu vurgularken, ya da birden fazla seçenek arasında serbest seçim olduğunu belirtirken kullanılır — özellikle çevrimiçi güvenlik tavsiyelerinde.

3 · Form

Positive

  • Whoever reported the scam did the right thing.
  • You can choose whichever browser you like, as long as you keep it updated.

Negative

  • No matter what he says, don't trust an unexpected money request.
  • Whatever excuse a scammer gives, it doesn't make the request safe.

Question

  • Whichever service will you use to report the fraud?
  • Does it matter whoever sent the message?

Short answers

  • It doesn't matter — no matter who it is, report it.
  • Whichever one — they all work.
Mistakes
  • Whoever is he, don't give him your password.Whoever he is, don't give him your password.

    After whoever, use normal subject–verb word order, not question order.

    TRWhoever + özne + fiil sırası normal cümle sırasını takip eder, soru sırasını değil.

  • You can use whatever password manager, it doesn't strong.You can use whatever password manager you like, as long as it's strong.

    Whatever is usually followed by a noun, or noun + subject + verb.

    TRWhatever'dan sonra genellikle bir isim ya da isim + özne + fiil gelir.

  • No matter what does he promise, ignore it.No matter what he promises, ignore it.

    After 'no matter what', don't use question word order — use normal statement order.

    TR'No matter what' den sonra yardımcı fiil devrik kullanılmaz, düz cümle yapısı gelir.

Compare
Whoever/Whatever/Whichever vs No Matter Who/What/Which
Whoever/Whatever/WhicheverNo matter who/what/which
Meaningany person/thing/one, freely chosenChoose whichever plan suits you.the same meaning, slightly more emphaticNo matter which plan you choose, read the terms first.
Positioncan start or be inside the clauseReport it to whoever is on duty.usually starts the clause, often with a commaNo matter who is on duty, report it.
Typical usegiving free choicegiving a warning that applies whatever happens

Expressions of Disapproval: Should(n't) Have, Ought to Have, It's Wrong To, I Don't Approve Of

1 · Notice
  • The company shouldn't have ignored so many warnings before the data breach happened.
  • He ought to have reported the scam the moment he noticed it, not a week later.
  • It's wrong to read someone else's private messages without their permission.
  • I don't approve of people who share other students' photos online without asking first.

Which expressions criticise a past action that already happened, and which express disapproval of behaviour in general?

2 · Discover

A Complete the rule

Complete the rule with the words in the box.

a mistake in the past that can't be changed nowin general, at any timea more formal alternative to shouldn't have

Well done — you can now tell past mistakes apart from general disapproval!

B Build sentences

Use the cues to build sentences of disapproval.

  1. the school shouldn't have / ignore the cyberbullying reports

    ✓ The school shouldn't have ignored the cyberbullying reports.

  2. the bank ought to have / warn customers about the phishing emails
    Model answer

    The bank ought to have warned customers about the phishing emails.

  3. it's wrong to / share someone's private photos without permission
    Model answer

    It's wrong to share someone's private photos without permission.

  4. I / don't approve of / people who / spread fake news online
    Model answer

    I don't approve of people who spread fake news online.

  5. they shouldn't have / click on that suspicious link
    Model answer

    They shouldn't have clicked on that suspicious link.

  6. ? / he / ought to have / report the hacker to the police
    Model answer

    Ought he to have reported the hacker to the police?

C Your turn

Write four sentences disapproving of unethical online behaviour, using should(n't) have, ought to have, it's wrong to, and I don't approve of.

He shouldn't have…They ought to have…It's wrong to…I don't approve of…
Rule

Should have / shouldn't have + past participle criticises a past mistake that can't be undone. Ought to have + past participle is a more formal alternative with the same meaning. It's wrong to + verb and I don't approve of + verb-ing express general disapproval of behaviour.

TRShould have / shouldn't have + V3 (geçmiş zaman ortacı), geçmişte yapılan ya da yapılmayan bir hatayı eleştirmek için kullanılır ve artık değiştirilemez. Ought to have + V3, 'shouldn't have' ile aynı anlamda ama daha resmi bir alternatiftir. It's wrong to + fiil ve I don't approve of + fiil-ing ise genel olarak bir davranışı onaylamadığımızı belirtmek için kullanılır.

When?

Use should(n't) have / ought to have to criticise a specific past mistake; use it's wrong to / I don't approve of to criticise unethical behaviour in general — especially when discussing cybercrime and online ethics.

TRGeçmişte olan bir hatayı eleştirirken should(n't) have / ought to have; genel olarak etik olmayan bir davranışı eleştirirken it's wrong to / I don't approve of kullanırız — özellikle siber suç ve çevrimiçi etik konularında.

3 · Form

Positive

  • He should have changed his password sooner.
  • It's wrong to hack into someone's account, even as a joke.

Negative

  • She shouldn't have opened that attachment.
  • I don't approve of people who spy on their friends' accounts.

Question

  • Should the company have informed its customers earlier?
  • Ought they to have reported the fraud immediately?

Short answers

  • Yes, they should have. / No, they shouldn't have.
  • Yes, they ought to. / No, they oughtn't to.
Mistakes
  • He should have go to the police.He should have gone to the police.

    Should have is followed by the past participle (V3), not the base form.

    TRShould have'den sonra V3 (past participle) gelir, base form değil.

  • It's wrong to shared private information.It's wrong to share private information.

    'It's wrong to' is followed by the to-infinitive (base form), not the past form.

    TR'It's wrong to' bir fiilin base (to-infinitive) hâliyle kullanılır.

  • I don't approve to people who bully others online.I don't approve of people who bully others online.

    'Approve' is followed by the preposition 'of', not 'to'.

    TR'Approve' fiilinden sonra gelen edat 'of'tur, 'to' değil.

Toneregret or criticism about one eventa general moral judgement

Bandits Without Borders

A century ago, a bandit needed a horse, a weapon and a lonely mountain road. Today's most successful criminals need none of these things — only a laptop, a stable internet connection and a convincing lie. Cybercrime has turned the whole planet into a kind of open territory, where a thief in one country can empty a stranger's bank account on the other side of the world without ever leaving their chair. Because these crimes cross borders so easily, catching the people responsible is far harder than catching a bandit on a single road.

Many cybercriminals don't break into computers at all — they trick people into opening the door themselves. This is known as phishing: sending a message that looks like it comes from a bank, a delivery company or even a friend, designed to make the reader click a link or hand over a password. The most dangerous scams don't rely on clever code; they rely on urgency and fear, pushing victims to act before they stop to think.

Fighting back doesn't always require expensive software. Simple habits — using a different password for every account, thinking twice before clicking an unexpected link, and turning on two-factor authentication — stop the vast majority of attacks before they start. Cyberspace may feel like bandit territory, but every careful user makes it a little less lawless.

Justice in the Digital Age

When a burglar breaks a window, the evidence is easy to see. When a hacker breaks into an account, the evidence is a string of invisible code, and that makes justice much harder to deliver. Courts around the world are now training specialist judges and detectives simply to keep up with crimes that didn't exist twenty years ago, from ransomware attacks on hospitals to the theft of millions of people's personal data in a single breach.

Poetic justice — the idea that wrongdoers eventually get the punishment they deserve, often in a fitting or ironic way — has always fascinated storytellers. In the digital world, this sometimes happens by accident: a scammer's own device gets infected by the same malware they used on others, or a hacker is caught because they bragged about the crime online. Real justice, however, rarely arrives so neatly; most cybercriminals are never identified at all.

That's why fairness in the digital age depends on more than punishment after the fact. Companies are expected to protect the data they collect, whistleblowers who expose wrongdoing deserve legal protection rather than punishment, and ordinary users need clear, simple laws they can actually understand. A just digital world isn't only about catching criminals — it's about building a system where trust is earned and protected in the first place.

2 While listening

BurakThat sounds suspicious. Did you click it?

EceI almost did, but then I noticed the sender's address was spelled wrong — it said 'bankk' with two k's.

BurakGood catch. That's a classic sign of a scam.

EceI know, right? I deleted it straight away.

Speaking
