Causative: Have/Get Something Done

1 · Notice
  • My grandfather never had his photo taken as a child — cameras were rare back then.
  • We're going to get the old family house repainted before the reunion this summer.
  • She doesn't cook every dish herself; she often has meals delivered by her granddaughter instead.
  • Could you get your brother to fix the Wi-Fi for your grandmother? She can't get online.

In these sentences, does the subject of the sentence do the action themselves, or does someone else do it for them?

2 · Discover

A Complete the rule

Look at the Notice sentences again. Complete the rule with the words in the box.

have/get + object + past participlesomeone else does it for usget + person + to + verb

Well done — you've worked out the rule yourself!

B Build sentences

Use the cues to build true sentences with the causative.

  1. my mother have / her hair / cut every month

    ✓ My mother has her hair cut every month.

  2. my grandparents get / their garden / looked after by a neighbour
    Model answer

    My grandparents get their garden looked after by a neighbour.

  3. we have / the old photos / scanned last weekend
    Model answer

    We had the old photos scanned last weekend.

  4. my father never / get / his car / washed he always does it himself
    Model answer

    My father never gets his car washed; he always does it himself.

  5. could you get / your cousin / to explain the new app to your grandmother?
    Model answer

    Could you get your cousin to explain the new app to your grandmother?

  6. ? / your family ever / have / a family portrait / painted
    Model answer

    Has your family ever had a family portrait painted?

C Your turn

Write four sentences about things your family has done for them, or things you could get a family member to do for you.

My grandparents have…Last year, we had…I'm going to get my…My parents never get…
Rule

We use 'have/get something done' when someone else does an action for us, instead of doing it ourselves: have/get + object + past participle (e.g. have your hair cut). We use 'get someone to do something' when we persuade or arrange for a specific person to do it (e.g. get your brother to fix the Wi-Fi).

TRBir işi kendimiz yapmak yerine başka birine yaptırdığımızda 'have/get something done' yapısını kullanırız: have/get + nesne + past participle (Ör: have your hair cut). Belirli bir kişiyi bir şey yapmaya ikna ettiğimizde ya da ayarladığımızda 'get someone to do something' yapısı kullanılır (Ör: get your brother to fix the Wi-Fi).

When?

We use these structures to talk about having a service done by someone else (getting a haircut, having a car washed) or asking a family member to do something for us.

TRBir hizmeti başkasına yaptırdığımızı (kuaförde saç kestirmek, arabayı yıkatmak) ya da bir aile üyesinden bir şey yapmasını istediğimizi anlatırken bu yapıları kullanırız.

3 · Form

Positive

  • She had her old letters framed for her birthday.
  • We got the garden tidied before the guests arrived.

Negative

  • He doesn't get his meals delivered; he cooks everything himself.
  • They haven't had the house repainted yet.

Question

  • Did you have your photo taken at the family reunion?
  • Can you get your uncle to explain the old family stories?

Short answers

  • Yes, I did. / No, I didn't.
  • Yes, I can. / No, he's too busy.
Mistakes
  • My grandmother had cut her hair yesterday.My grandmother had her hair cut yesterday.

    In the causative, the verb must be a past participle placed after the object: have + object + past participle.

    TRCausative yapıda fiil past participle olmalı ve nesneden sonra gelmelidir: have + nesne + past participle.

  • He got his brother fix the computer.He got his brother to fix the computer.

    In 'get someone to do something', the 'to' must not be dropped.

    TR'Get someone to do something' yapısında 'to' unutulmamalıdır.

  • We had done our photos taken last month.We had our photos taken last month.

    In the causative, an extra 'done' must not be added; only have/get + object + past participle is used.

    TRCausative yapıda 'done' fazladan eklenmemelidir; sadece have/get + nesne + past participle kullanılır.

Asking for and Giving Advice: should, ought to, had better, if I were you, why don't you

1 · Notice
  • If you want a closer relationship with your grandparents, you should call them more often, not just text.
  • You ought to listen to your parents' experience before dismissing their advice completely.
  • You had better ask your grandmother about the family recipe before it's forgotten forever.
  • Why don't you ask your father to teach you how he learned to save money as a teenager?

Which expressions give general advice, and which one sounds like a stronger warning about something urgent?

2 · Discover

A Complete the rule

Complete the rule with the words in the box.

general advicea stronger warning, often with a negative consequencea friendly suggestion, as a questionimagining yourself in someone's situation

Great — you've worked out the rule!

B Build sentences

Use the cues to build true or realistic advice sentences. Choose the most suitable expression.

  1. you should / spend more time with your grandparents

    ✓ You should spend more time with your grandparents.

  2. you ought to / respect your elders' experience
    Model answer

    You ought to respect your elders' experience.

  3. you had better / apologise before your grandmother gets upset
    Model answer

    You had better apologise before your grandmother gets upset.

  4. why / don't you ask your father for advice about the interview?
    Model answer

    Why don't you ask your father for advice about the interview?

  5. if I / were you I / would talk to my parents honestly
    Model answer

    If I were you, I would talk to my parents honestly.

  6. ? / what / should I do if my grandparents disagree with my choices?
    Model answer

    What should I do if my grandparents disagree with my choices?

C Your turn

Write four sentences giving advice about handling a generational disagreement — use at least three different advice expressions.

If your grandparents don't understand…, you should…You ought to…Why don't you…If I were you, I would…
Rule

'Should' and 'ought to' give general advice. 'Had better' gives a stronger warning, often implying a negative consequence if not followed. 'Why don't you…?' offers a friendly suggestion as a question. 'If I were you, I would…' gives advice by imagining yourself in someone else's situation.

TRGenel tavsiye vermek için 'should' ve 'ought to' kullanılır. Daha güçlü, ciddi sonuçları olan bir uyarı için 'had better' kullanılır. Nazik bir öneriyi soru şeklinde sunmak için 'Why don't you…?' kullanılır. Kendimizi birinin yerine koyarak tavsiye vermek için 'If I were you, I would…' kullanılır.

When?

We use these expressions when exchanging advice within a family or across generations, especially when a polite or respectful tone is needed.

TRAile içinde ya da kuşaklar arasında tavsiye alışverişinde bulunurken, özellikle nazik ya da saygılı bir üslup gerektiğinde bu yapıları kullanırız.

3 · Form

Positive

  • You should call your grandmother more often.
  • You had better talk to your parents before deciding.

Negative

  • You shouldn't ignore your elders' advice completely.
  • You'd better not argue with your grandfather at dinner.

Question

  • What should I do about this disagreement?
  • Why don't you ask your mother for her opinion?

Short answers

  • You should apologise. / You shouldn't wait any longer.
  • Why don't you? / I already did.
Mistakes
  • You should to listen to your grandmother.You should listen to your grandmother.

    'Should' is a modal verb and is never followed by 'to'.

    TR'Should' bir modal fiildir ve sonrasında 'to' gelmez.

  • You had better to apologise soon.You had better apologise soon.

    'Had better' is never followed by 'to' — it takes the base verb directly.

    TR'Had better' sonrasında 'to' kullanılmaz, doğrudan fiil gelir.

  • If I was you, I would talk to him honestly.If I were you, I would talk to him honestly.

    In this advice structure, 'were' is used instead of 'was', even with 'I' (a hypothetical condition).

    TRTavsiye verirken bu yapıda 'was' değil 'were' kullanılır (hypothetical/unreal condition).

The Same River, Different Banks

Every generation likes to believe it invented independence. Grandparents in Türkiye often describe childhoods spent playing football in the street until dark, with no phone to call home and no need for one — the whole neighbourhood, they say, looked after every child. Their grandchildren, meanwhile, grew up with smartphones in their pockets and a constant stream of messages from worried parents. Which generation was really freer is a question that rarely gets a simple answer.

Sociologists suggest that each generation's outlook is shaped less by personality than by the economic and technological conditions it grows up in. A generation that lived through hardship often values saving money and repairing things rather than replacing them; a generation raised with instant information often values speed, choice and self-expression instead. Neither outlook is wrong — they are simply sensible responses to very different worlds.

The real danger, researchers argue, isn't the generation gap itself but the assumption that it can't be bridged. Families who regularly talk about their different experiences — rather than judging each other by their own generation's standards — tend to pass down both practical skills and emotional closeness. A grandmother who gets her granddaughter to show her how video calls work, and a granddaughter who asks her grandmother how she once cooked for a family of ten on a tight budget, are both quietly closing the same gap.

Letters, Landlines and Loud Dinners

Merve's grandfather still keeps a box of handwritten letters from his years working abroad in the 1970s. 'We waited weeks for news,' he tells her, 'and every letter felt like an event.' Merve, who video-calls her cousins in Germany every weekend without a second thought, finds the story almost impossible to imagine — yet she notices that her grandfather remembers the exact words of letters he received fifty years ago, while she sometimes forgets a text message within minutes.

The family's Sunday dinners are where these two worlds collide loudest. Merve's grandmother insists that everyone sit at the table with their phones switched off, a rule she enforces with surprising firmness for someone so soft-spoken otherwise. At first, Merve found the rule old-fashioned and slightly unfair. Over time, though, she noticed that these were the only two hours each week when her whole extended family actually looked at one another while talking.

Merve's mother, caught between both generations, often acts as a translator of sorts — explaining to her own mother why a video call matters as much as a letter once did, and reminding Merve that patience, not just connection, was something her grandfather's generation had in abundance. Nobody in the family has fully convinced the others that their way is best. What they have managed, slowly, is to borrow a little from each generation and build something that works for all three.

Speaking

Bridging the Gap

In pairs, discuss the biggest differences between your generation and your grandparents' generation. Use the causative (have/get something done) and at least two advice expressions (should, ought to, had better, why don't you, if I were you) where natural.

Speaking

A Value Worth Passing Down

Prepare a two-minute talk about one national, moral or cultural value (such as respect for elders, hospitality, honesty or family loyalty) that has been passed down in your family. Explain what it is, how it's shown in daily life, and why it matters to you.

Writing

Opinion Essay: Is the Generation Gap Growing?

Write a short opinion essay answering the question: is the generation gap growing wider today, or can it be bridged? Start with a clear topic sentence stating your opinion, add at least three supporting sentences with reasons and examples, and finish with a concluding sentence.
