Mixed Conditionals: Linking a Past Decision to a Present Situation

1 · Notice
  • If she hadn't moved to the mountains ten years ago, she wouldn't be living this simply now.
  • If he hadn't given up his office job, he wouldn't be growing his own vegetables today.
  • They wouldn't own this cabin now if they hadn't taken the risk five years ago.
  • If she hadn't inherited the family land, would she be living this unconventional life today?

In each sentence, does the 'if' part refer to the past or the present? What about the result?

2 · Discover

A Complete the rule

Complete the rule with the words in the box.

the pastthe presentwould/wouldn't + base verbhad/hadn't + past participle

Exactly — you've worked out how mixed conditionals connect past decisions to present situations!

B Build sentences

Use the cues to build true mixed conditional sentences.

  1. she not move to the countryside she / not know how to grow vegetables now

    ✓ If she hadn't moved to the countryside, she wouldn't know how to grow vegetables now.

  2. he not leave his office job he / not have this much free time now
    Model answer

    If he hadn't left his office job, he wouldn't have this much free time now.

  3. they not build their own cabin they / not be living rent-free today
    Model answer

    If they hadn't built their own cabin, they wouldn't be living rent-free today.

  4. she not learn to fish and forage she / not survive alone in the forest now
    Model answer

    If she hadn't learned to fish and forage, she wouldn't survive alone in the forest now.

  5. we not decide to downsize we / not feel this free now
    Model answer

    If we hadn't decided to downsize, we wouldn't feel this free now.

  6. ? / she not inherit the family land she / live this unconventional life today
    Model answer

    If she hadn't inherited the family land, would she be living this unconventional life today?

C Your turn

Write four mixed conditional sentences about your own life, or about someone you know who made an unusual choice, linking a past decision to a present situation.

If I hadn't…If my family hadn't…She wouldn't be… now if…We wouldn't have… today if…
Rule

Mixed conditionals connect a past decision or action to a present situation. The 'if' clause refers to the past (had/hadn't + past participle); the result clause refers to the present (would/wouldn't + base verb).

TRMixed conditionals (karışık koşul cümleleri), geçmişteki bir kararın şimdiki bir durum üzerindeki etkisini anlatmak için kullanılır. 'If' cümleciğinde geçmişe atıfta bulunmak için 'had/hadn't + past participle' kullanılır; sonuç cümleciğinde ise şimdiki zamana atıfta bulunmak için 'would/wouldn't + base verb' kullanılır.

When?

We use this structure to talk about how a past decision — such as moving, leaving a job, or choosing a lifestyle — still affects someone's present living conditions.

TRGeçmişte alınan bir kararın (taşınma, bir işten ayrılma, bir yaşam biçimi seçme gibi) bugünkü yaşam koşulları üzerindeki etkisini anlatırken bu yapıyı kullanırız.

3 · Form

Positive

  • If she hadn't moved off-grid, she wouldn't be living this simply now.
  • If he hadn't taken the risk, he wouldn't own this land today.

Negative

  • If they hadn't downsized, they wouldn't feel this free now.
  • If we hadn't left the city, we wouldn't be growing our own food today.

Question

  • Would you be living differently now if you had made a different choice?
  • If she hadn't inherited the land, would she be doing this today?

Short answers

  • Yes, I would. / No, I wouldn't.
  • Yes, she would. / No, she wouldn't.
Mistakes
  • If she didn't move to the mountains, she wouldn't be living this simply now.If she hadn't moved to the mountains, she wouldn't be living this simply now.

    When the 'if' clause refers to a past action, use 'had + past participle', not the Simple Past.

    TRGeçmişteki bir eylemden bahsederken 'if' cümleciğinde Simple Past değil, 'had + past participle' kullanılmalıdır.

  • If he hadn't left his job, he won't have this free time today.If he hadn't left his job, he wouldn't have this free time today.

    The result clause needs 'would', not 'will', because it links to an unreal present situation.

    TRSonuç cümleciğinde şimdiki zamana atıfta bulunurken 'will' değil 'would' kullanılır.

  • If they hadn't downsized, they wouldn't have felt this free now.If they hadn't downsized, they wouldn't feel this free now.

    When the result refers to the present, use 'would + base verb', not 'would have + past participle'.

    TRSonuç şimdiki zamana ait olduğunda 'would have + past participle' değil, 'would + base verb' kullanılır.

Compare
Mixed Conditional vs Third Conditional
Mixed (past cause → present result)Third Conditional (past cause → past result)
Time of resultnow / todayIf she hadn't moved, she wouldn't be living here now.in the past onlyIf she hadn't moved, she wouldn't have met her husband.
Result clause formwould + base verbwould have + past participle
Usea past decision still affects the presenta past decision affected a past event, now finished

Relative Clauses: Describing Unusual People, Places and Lifestyles

1 · Notice
  • A digital nomad is someone who works online while travelling the world.
  • The valley, which has no roads or electricity, is home to just three families.
  • This is the cabin where the family spent their first off-grid winter.
  • Elena, whose entire house runs on solar power, hasn't paid an electricity bill in years.

In which sentence does the relative clause give extra, non-essential information (with commas), and in which sentences is it essential to identify who or what we mean?

2 · Discover

A Complete the rule

Complete the rule with the words in the box.

defining (no commas, essential)non-defining (with commas, extra info)who/thatwhich/thatwherewhose

Great work — you've worked out how to identify and add extra information with relative clauses!

B Build sentences

Combine the two sentences using a relative clause.

  1. A hermit is a person. This person chooses to live alone.

    ✓ A hermit is a person who chooses to live alone.

  2. Mira built her own house from recycled materials. Mira now teaches other people to do the same.
    Model answer

    Mira, who built her own house from recycled materials, now teaches other people to do the same.

  3. The commune grows all of its own food. The commune has around forty members.
    Model answer

    The commune, which grows all of its own food, has around forty members.

  4. This is the mountain hut. The family spent three winters there.
    Model answer

    This is the mountain hut where the family spent three winters.

  5. Tomas gave up his salary to become a shepherd. Tomas's flock now provides everything his family needs.
    Model answer

    Tomas, whose flock now provides everything his family needs, gave up his salary to become a shepherd.

  6. ? / Do you know anyone. This person has chosen an unconventional lifestyle.
    Model answer

    Do you know anyone who has chosen an unconventional lifestyle?

C Your turn

Write four sentences describing unusual people, places or lifestyles using relative clauses (who, which, where, whose).

A digital nomad is someone who…I'd love to visit a place where…I know someone whose…An off-grid home is a house which…
Rule

Defining relative clauses (who, which, where, whose, that) are essential to identify who or what we mean and are not separated by commas. Non-defining relative clauses add extra information, are separated by commas, can be removed without changing the core meaning, and never use 'that'.

TRDefining relative clause'lar (who, which, where, whose, that), kimden ya da neyden bahsettiğimizi belirlemek için gereklidir ve virgülle ayrılmaz. Non-defining relative clause'lar ise ekstra bilgi verir, virgülle ayrılır ve cümleden çıkarılabilir; bu tür cümlelerde 'that' kullanılmaz.

When?

We use relative clauses to identify or add extra information about people, places or lifestyles.

TRİnsanları, yerleri ya da yaşam tarzlarını tanımlarken ya da hakkında ek bilgi verirken relative clause kullanırız.

3 · Form

Positive

  • A hermit is someone who lives alone, away from other people.
  • The valley, which has no roads, can only be reached on foot.

Negative

  • This isn't a lifestyle which everyone could manage.
  • He doesn't live in a place where phones even work.

Question

  • Do you know anyone whose life is completely off-grid?
  • Is this the cabin where they spent the winter?

Short answers

  • Yes, I do. / No, I don't.
  • Yes, it is. / No, it isn't.
Mistakes
  • A digital nomad is someone which works online.A digital nomad is someone who works online.

    Use 'who' (or 'that') for people, not 'which'.

    TRİnsanlar için 'who' (ya da 'that') kullanılır, 'which' kullanılmaz.

  • The valley, that has no roads, is very isolated.The valley, which has no roads, is very isolated.

    'That' cannot be used in non-defining (comma) relative clauses; use 'which' or 'who' instead.

    TRNon-defining relative clause'larda (virgüllü) 'that' kullanılmaz; 'which' ya da 'who' kullanılır.

  • This is the cabin which they spent their first winter.This is the cabin where they spent their first winter.

    Use 'where' for places, because the clause is missing a preposition (in/at) that 'which' alone wouldn't supply.

    TRBir yerden bahsederken, cümlede zaten bir edat (in/at) eksik olduğu için 'where' kullanılır.

Defining (no commas)Non-defining (with commas)

Can you remove it?no — the sentence loses its meaningyes — the sentence still makes sense

The Family Who Left the Grid

Six years ago, Aylin and her husband sold their flat in the city, bought four hectares of rocky hillside, and moved into a tent while they built a house with their own hands. If they hadn't made that decision, they would still be commuting two hours a day and paying rent they could barely afford. Today they grow almost all of their own vegetables, keep chickens and goats, and generate their own electricity from a small solar array on the roof.

Life off-grid is not simply a romantic escape, however. Aylin describes the first winter as brutal: pipes froze, the solar panels produced almost no power on cloudy days, and there were weeks when the nearest working shop felt impossibly far away. 'We had to become resourceful fast,' she says. 'If we hadn't learned to fix things ourselves, we wouldn't have survived that winter.' Slowly, through trial and error, the family adapted.

What surprises visitors most is not the hardship but how ordinary the family's days now feel. Aylin no longer misses her old commute, and her children, who grew up thinking every home has running water on tap, now understand exactly where their food and power come from. 'People ask if we regret it,' she says. 'Honestly, if we hadn't taken the risk, I don't think we'd be half as happy as we are now.'

The Keeper of the Last Light

On a narrow rock eighteen kilometres from the nearest coastline stands a lighthouse where Meltem has spent every summer for the past nine years. She is one of the very few keepers left whose job hasn't been fully replaced by automatic lights and remote sensors. For four months at a time, her only regular company is the sound of the wind, the occasional passing ship, and a radio link to the mainland.

The work itself is precise and demanding: checking the lamp mechanism, logging weather conditions, and keeping meticulous records that shipping companies still rely on. 'People imagine I just sit and read novels all day,' Meltem laughs, 'but a lighthouse that isn't properly maintained is a lighthouse that eventually fails — and ships depend on that light.' Supplies arrive by boat once a fortnight, weather permitting; when storms close in, they simply don't arrive.

What keeps Meltem returning each year is not tradition alone. She describes a quality of attention that isolated life demands — noticing the smallest change in the sky, the tide, the sound of the generator — that she has never found anywhere else. 'In the city, I moved through my days without really seeing them,' she explains. 'Out here, on a rock where almost nothing changes quickly, I finally learned to pay attention.'

InterviewerSena, you've been living off-grid in the village for three years now. How do you get water and electricity?

SenaWe get our electricity from solar panels, and our water comes from a well we dug ourselves.

InterviewerNo mains connection at all?

SenaNone. But I think I wouldn't appreciate hot showers nearly as much if I hadn't moved to the village and lived without them for a while.

InterviewerDo you ever feel lonely out there?

SenaHonestly, I'm not really lonely. The solitude is actually part of why I stayed — I love the quiet.

InterviewerWas moving here always the plan?

SenaNot at all! If I hadn't taken my first trip to the village, I'd still be living in my old flat in the city, honestly.

InterviewerWhat changed your mind?

SenaThat first visit. I just fell in love with the simplicity of it.
